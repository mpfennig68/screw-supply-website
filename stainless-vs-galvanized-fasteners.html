Stainless vs. Galvanized Fasteners for Outdoor and Marine Work

“Outdoor” can describe a sign bracket that dries after a rainstorm, a cabinet beside a salted road, or a fixture that gets splashed at a marina. Those are three different buying decisions. Before you quote the fasteners, find out what’s likely to get the joint wet, what it will touch, and how hard it will be to replace.

When 304 stainless makes sense

Type 304 is a reasonable starting point when the job calls for stainless and exposure is mainly to rain or humidity, with little salt. Take a close look at sheltered areas, seams, and recesses. A fastener under an overhang might stay out of the rain but still collect deposits that rain would otherwise wash away. At a site near the coast or a salted road, that detail can change the material choice. IMOA exterior stainless guide (PDF)

When 316 stainless earns its place

Type 316 contains molybdenum, which improves resistance to chloride-related corrosion. Put it on the shortlist when hardware will see coastal salt air or deicing spray. For a fixture that gets direct saltwater splash, stays wet in a tight joint, or sits underwater, have your engineer evaluate the exposure and alloy. Severe marine service can call for a more corrosion-resistant grade than 316. IMOA stainless grade FAQ

Think about the return visit before choosing between acceptable materials. Replacing a screw at a workbench is one thing. Sending a crew back to a waterfront site, opening a cabinet, or taking a fixture out of service is another. Where salt exposure favors 316, the work involved in replacing the fastener can be a good reason to specify it up front.

Where hot-dip galvanized steel fits

Hot-dip galvanizing puts a bonded zinc coating on steel. The coating acts as a barrier, and the zinc corrodes preferentially to help protect the steel beneath it. If you’re fastening galvanized brackets or framing, compatible hot-dip galvanized hardware is a sensible option to evaluate within the job’s requirements. American Galvanizers Association fastener guide

Spell out hot-dip galvanized when that’s what the drawing requires. Ordinary zinc plating generally produces a thinner coating. For a threaded assembly, confirm that the bolt, nut, and washers are specified to work together. The coating affects thread fit, so a bolt and nut with the same nominal thread size still need a compatibility check. American Galvanizers Association threaded assembly guidance

Check what the fastener will touch

Galvanic corrosion can occur when dissimilar metals are electrically connected and a conductive liquid, such as saltwater, bridges them. The relative surface areas matter, too. A small galvanized fastener against a large stainless plate can lose its zinc coating more quickly under those conditions.

Look at both sides of the joint. Record the material and finish of the panel, bracket, nut, and washer. If the design calls for mixed metals in a wet location, ask your engineer whether the joint needs electrical isolation or a different material pairing. American Galvanizers Association guidance on mixed metals

Put the exposure on the RFQ

A buyer can save a round of questions by sending four details with the part list:

  • Exposure. Say whether the joint sees rain, deicing spray, coastal air, direct splash, or immersion.
  • Mating materials. Identify the metals and coatings the fastener will contact.
  • Fastener specification. Include the grade or coating, strength requirement, thread, length, head style, and quantity.
  • Service access. Say who needs to remove the fastener and whether you need matching bits, drivers, or sockets.

Confirm the material, coating, strength, and installation requirements with the manufacturer or your engineer before ordering.
